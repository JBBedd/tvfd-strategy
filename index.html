About this plan

TVFD One, Three, and Five Year Strategic Plan

Board discussion draft for November 4, 2026

Triana Volunteer Fire Department protects the Town of Triana and the surrounding Madison County district from its station on 6th Street: five paid staff (one full-time, four part-time) and about 20 volunteers providing fire suppression, emergency medical response, and technical rescue. The department is an independent 501(c)(3) nonprofit, governed by its own board. Its on-site training facility hosts Alabama Fire College courses for Triana members and neighboring departments alike.

This plan covers three horizons on the department’s October 1 to September 30 fiscal year: Year 1 (FY2026-27, through September 2027), Year 3 (through September 2029), and Year 5 (through September 2031). It is a board discussion draft for the Annual Membership Meeting season. It does not authorize a purchase, financing, a new paid position, or a bylaw amendment; each of those requires its own board action on a complete proposal.

The growth we are living in

Triana is one of the fastest-growing communities in Madison County, and this department is being rebuilt in the middle of that growth. The first-due district is home to close to 7,000 residents today. It has nearly doubled since 2020 alone, and approved development carries it toward about 7,700 residents at buildout. The town itself counted fewer than 500 residents at the 2000 census and counts about 6,500 today. New subdivisions are rising alongside the working farmland, Tennessee River frontage and canal banks, conservancy and Tennessee Valley Authority refuge land, and wooded acreage that have always defined this place. That mix makes wildland-urban interface fire the defining local hazard: the places where new rooftops meet fields, riverbanks, and woods are exactly where Triana calls come from. The district borders Redstone Arsenal and sits near Huntsville International Airport, which adds commuter traffic, industrial neighbors, and mutual aid obligations to the workload.

The pattern is the district’s growth arriving as workload: volume climbed from a partial-year 163 calls in late 2024 to 417 in 2025, and 2026 is on pace for roughly 460. The plan’s staffing and apparatus logic is built on this trend, and the mix explains the weight EMS readiness and medical supplies carry in the operating detail: with two-thirds of calls medical, medical readiness is the daily work, not the exception. A county archive of run statistics for 2019 through 2023 exists; extracting it and reconciling it with First Due into one multi-year, NFIRS-consistent call history is a November deliverable in the 90-day launch table, and the reconciled series becomes the standing data pack for applications and planning. The service results in the table above improved while the load climbed, and protecting them is a stated Year 1 priority.

The department itself is part of the growth story. In 2024 it had two members. Today it has 25: five paid staff and about 20 volunteers, neighbors responding to neighbors. Its self-built training facility now hosts Alabama Fire College courses for neighboring departments, a regional role that did not exist two years ago. Growth is therefore not background in this plan; it is the load the plan is sized for. Year 1 protects coverage quality while volume climbs. Year 3 tests the coverage and staffing model against the buildout trajectory. Year 5 answers the deployment questions with data, through gates, rather than assuming growth automatically justifies a bigger department.

Recommended direction

Three moves organize everything that follows.

First, secure a dependable reserve-response solution (a reserve fire engine, ready when the frontline engine is down) without consuming the money needed to operate safely. The reserve engine decision is gated on inspection, an all-in delivered cost, and written funding eligibility, and it proceeds only when a funding source is identified and confirmed in writing.

Second, run the department on one verified view of its money. Town support, County allocations, and nonprofit cash are tracked in a single management picture with each source’s restrictions visible, so no decision is made from an unverified balance or from a pooled total that mixes restricted and unrestricted funds.

Third, make the unglamorous work routine: retention and scheduling, preventive maintenance and inspection calendars, a monthly financial close, and a named owner and backup for every essential function.

Grants and fundraising accelerate this plan; they do not carry it. Essential response must remain viable when awards are delayed or denied, so no pending grant, projected event, or hoped-for appropriation is counted in the funding baseline anywhere in this plan.

Funding basis

Chief Chad Beddingfield joined the department in June 2026. This plan draws on department budget records across the last four fiscal years. The pattern is a department whose public funding has grown quickly and whose costs are dominated by people and the station. The board’s own working structure supports the plan: the board meets on the first Tuesday of each month at 6:00 PM, the Equipment and Capacity Planning Committee established in October 2026 (Chief coordinating) owns the ranked and costed needs list, and the Fundraising Committee has restarted under Michael Owens.

Town funding carries a reliability limit the board should plan around. An approved Town budget is not a guarantee that every line reaches the department: the Town always pays the station mortgage and employee payroll and benefits, and a few other Town-side items, and those are the dependable core of Town support. Town support will not increase beyond that core, and this plan counts Town support at the core and no more. Other approved Town lines do not always materialize, because the Town revisits its budget during the year, so nothing beyond the core is counted until it is received. The $20,000 training, education, and equipment allocation in the Town request is not expected, and it is counted at $0 in every figure in this plan.

Almost none of this money passes through the department's own bank accounts. Madison County's operating support is paid from the county fire mill tax by purchase order: the department submits purchase orders, the County purchases, and those funds never pass through the department's hands. The Town pays payroll and station costs directly. The corporation's own recurring income is a single line: the County appropriation for the part-time administrative support role (a 1099 role), $25,000 in FY2026-27; beyond it, only small Forestry amounts and occasional donations move through TVFD accounts. The corporation runs thin cash by design, not neglect: its own accounts were never meant to hold the department's budget. The nonprofit corporation's own cash at November 3, 2026 was $5,126.78 across three accounts (Forestry $12.22; ISO $990.56; Misc Ops $4,124.00), and some of that cash is restricted by source. In October 2026 the nonprofit's own accounts recorded about $4,344.72 of spending against no revenue, and a $4,000 Madison County support payment received at the start of November brought total nonprofit cash to the $5,126.78 above. Departmental support totals in this plan are therefore a management view of all resources behind the mission, never a statement of cash the corporation can spend or of revenue the corporation receives.

USDA Community Facilities is a federal route the department’s October research examined in detail. Improving our ISO rating

The department’s current Public Protection Classification is 6/6X, scored at 43.51 of 105.50 points in the 2023 ISO evaluation. The component picture shows where the points sit. Emergency communications scored 9.08 of 10 and is near maxed, and community risk reduction earned 3.65 of 5.50 bonus points. The fire department components are thin: company personnel, the staffing credit, scored 2.78 of 15; engine companies 1.50 of 6; and reserve pumpers 0.03 of 0.50. Water supply scored 16.34 of 40 and is the structural gate: hydrant flows average about 928 gallons per minute with none reaching 1,500, a water tower is offline, the supply is purchased water, and the mains are a mix that includes asbestos pipe. The grading also adjusts for the gap between the fire department and water supply scores, and that adjustment shrinks only as the weaker side improves.

Most of what can be earned without new infrastructure is documentation and disciplined operations. Documented training is the single biggest controllable lever: rosters, hours, and lesson plans mapped to recognized standards, because ISO gives no training credit where records do not exist. Apparatus and equipment testing (pump, hose, and ladder) performed on schedule and logged earns and protects credit. Formalized automatic aid adds creditable personnel on working incidents: the department already responds on automatic aid with Huntsville Fire & Rescue on structure fires, and this plan documents and strengthens that arrangement. Hydrant flow testing and records protect the water credit the district already has, and a tanker-shuttle capability with documented alternative supply adds what the hydrants cannot. Community risk reduction work (pre-incident plans, inspections, and public education, all recorded) is among the easiest incremental points available.

The plan’s goal is PPC 4 within the plan horizon, earned as a documentation-and-operations achievement. PPC 3 is named honestly as gated on water infrastructure and staffing levels this plan does not control, and it is not promised. Year 1 starts the work with two actions: a Verisk pre-evaluation to pin the department’s current point positions before numeric targets are locked, supported by the department’s ISO consultant, and an ISO evidence binder, maintained by the ISO Coordinator, that collects training, testing, aid, and risk-reduction records as they are created. The reserve engine speaks to the reserve pumper credit and the training records system to the training credit, and both are pursued first for readiness. Purchases in this plan are justified by readiness and service life; none is sold on a promised ISO improvement.

Operating reserve policy

The corporate operating reserve is unrestricted cash deliberately designated for unexpected operating disruption. Donor-restricted gifts, grant balances, County-held appropriations, unpaid reimbursements, and pass-through obligations are excluded, and no balance is counted in both the operating reserve and the capital provision. The starting point is the department’s actual cash behavior: a month of spending with no receipts is normal in the nonprofit’s own accounts, so the reserve is sized to real obligations, not to the departmental support totals. Year 1 milestone: build $6,000 of verified unrestricted operating reserve, funded only from net fundraising remaining after any operating shortfall is met, and from other unrestricted receipts; if receipts land below that, the milestone scales down rather than drawing on dollars already committed elsewhere. Within 90 days, replace that starting figure with a documented target equal to three months of the nonprofit’s true recurring cash obligations, plus the largest expected 90-day reimbursement bridge and a board-selected emergency allowance. Any use of the reserve identifies the purpose, the authorizer, the amount, and the replenishment schedule.

Reducing single-person dependency

Each essential process (bank access, reconciliations, grant registrations, procurement files, records) is documented, has role-based access, and has a trained backup; one handoff is tested each quarter. Passwords are never shared informally to solve a continuity problem.

Expansion gates

Growth in the district does not, by itself, authorize growth in the department. Before TVFD adds a responder vehicle, a new capability such as water rescue, a major power system, a paid position, or a facility project, the proposal presented to the board includes: the defined need; a Chief-approved operating concept; the people and qualifications required; acquisition cost and recurring cost; full lifecycle cost; the permitted funding source; insurance and maintenance arrangements; and the effect on existing readiness. Whether a district approaching 7,700 residents warrants deployment changes, expanded paid coverage, or facility planning is a study question for the Year 3 and Year 5 reviews, answered with geographic and time-of-day data and passed through these gates. It is never an assumption built into this plan’s numbers. A concern raised in discussion is risk-assessment work first, not a purchasing approval.
